Executive summary

The opportunity in brief

The market
Iraq holds the world's fifth-largest proven oil reserves and is OPEC's second-largest producer. Oil funds around 90% of government revenue, and the country is pursuing a major build-out across upstream, gas, refining, power and — increasingly — renewables.
The core opportunity
Upstream expansion, gas capture to cut flaring, new refineries, a large and persistent electricity deficit driving generation and transmission investment, and a growing solar pipeline toward roughly 12 GW by 2030.
The buyers
The Ministry of Oil and its state companies, the Ministry of Electricity, the National and provincial Investment Commissions, and — in the north — the Kurdistan Region's own energy authorities.
The barriers to entry
Separate Federal and Kurdistan regimes, contract and payment structures, registration and customs, security and logistics that vary by field and governorate, and project timing tied to budgets and OPEC quotas.

Market overview

The backbone of Iraq's economy

Energy is the foundation of Iraq's economy: crude oil export revenue accounts for roughly 90% of government income. With about 145 billion barrels of proven reserves and production near 4.4 million barrels a day, Iraq is OPEC's second-largest producer, and its oil ministry has set out plans to raise capacity toward 6 million barrels a day later this decade.

But the sector is far broader than crude. Iraq flares large volumes of associated gas for lack of processing capacity — a major opportunity in itself — while new refineries such as Karbala expand domestic capacity, and a persistent electricity deficit is driving heavy investment in generation, transmission and, increasingly, solar power. For international energy companies, from oil-service majors to power and renewable developers, Iraq is one of the region's largest energy markets, with substantial unmet demand across gas processing, power infrastructure and specialist services.

Key demand drivers

What is pushing the market

A large electricity deficit

Peak demand runs well ahead of reliable supply, driving sustained investment in generation and transmission.

Associated-gas utilisation

Capturing gas currently flared, to fuel power plants and cut waste, is a national priority and a standalone opportunity.

Upstream expansion

Plans to lift oil capacity toward 6 million bpd require field development, drilling and services at scale.

New refining capacity

Refineries such as Karbala, and expansions elsewhere, aim to cut fuel imports and add value at home.

Grid expansion & interconnection

Transmission upgrades and cross-border interconnections to stabilise and grow supply.

The renewables push

A growing pipeline of large solar projects targeting roughly 12 GW by 2030 opens the market to new players.

These demand drivers provide a useful framework, but every opportunity still requires project-level commercial and regulatory assessment.

Buyers & decision-makers

Who actually awards the work

Iraq's energy sector is led by powerful state institutions. Identifying which body — and which company — holds authority over a given field or project is essential before investing effort.

  • The Ministry of Oil, setting policy and leading upstream, gas and refining.
  • The Ministry of Electricity, responsible for generation, transmission and distribution.
  • Basra Oil Company, North Oil Company, Midland Oil Company and Missan Oil Company — the regional state operators.
  • The State Company for Oil Projects (SCOP) and the Oil Pipelines Company.
  • The National Investment Commission and provincial investment commissions, for licence projects.
  • The Kurdistan Region's Ministry of Natural Resources, under its separate regime.
Knowing which state company controls a field or project — and who inside it can commit — is exactly where local knowledge earns its value.
Procurement & contracting routes

How to actually win work

There are several ways into Iraq's energy sector, depending on whether you are an operator, service company, EPC contractor, technology supplier or developer.

Technical service contracts

The traditional route for international oil companies operating Iraq's producing fields.

Licensing rounds

Periodic bid rounds for exploration and development blocks issued by the Ministry of Oil.

Government tenders

Public tenders from ministries and state companies for works, services and supply.

EPC / EPCM / EPCF

Engineering and construction packages, with management or financing variants for larger schemes.

PPP & BOT

Public-private and build-operate-transfer structures, increasingly used for power and gas.

Independent power (IPP)

Independent power and solar projects developed and operated under long-term offtake.

Joint venture

A JV with a state company or Iraqi partner, combining capability with local access.

Supply & technology partner

Supplying equipment, systems or technology directly or through distributors and main contractors.

Market entry risks

What to plan around

A realistic view of the risks is part of doing this well. None of these are reasons to avoid Iraq — they are factors to plan around, and most are manageable with the right preparation and partners.

  • Two distinct regimes — Federal Iraq and the Kurdistan Region — with different laws, contracts and tender systems.
  • Payment terms, guarantees and cash-flow structures that must be planned before, not during, a contract.
  • Company registration, tax, customs and import processes that reward preparation and local guidance.
  • Security and logistics that vary by field and governorate and require sensible movement planning.
  • Compliance and sanctions screening that need careful, up-front attention.
  • Project timing affected by budget cycles and, for oil, OPEC production quotas.
Being honest about these is deliberate: the firms that succeed in Iraq are the ones that plan around its realities rather than around headlines.

FAQ

Common questions

Who awards energy projects in Iraq?

Oil and gas projects are led by the Ministry of Oil and its state companies (Basra Oil Company, North Oil Company and others); power projects by the Ministry of Electricity; and investment-licence projects through the National and provincial Investment Commissions. The Kurdistan Region has its own ministry and regime.

Can foreign companies work in Iraq's oil and gas sector?

Yes. International oil companies, service companies and EPC contractors are active across Iraq's fields, typically through technical service contracts, licensing rounds, EPC contracts or partnerships with state companies, and by registering locally.

Is a local partner required for energy projects in Iraq?

A local partner is not always legally required, but a vetted Iraqi partner, agent or subcontractor is often essential in practice for tendering, permits, mobilisation and working with the Ministry of Oil, the Ministry of Electricity and state companies.

What are the biggest opportunities in Iraq's energy sector?

Upstream oilfield development and services, gas capture and processing to cut flaring, new and expanded refineries, power generation and transmission to close the electricity deficit, and a growing pipeline of solar and renewable projects.

What is Iraq doing about its electricity shortage?

Iraq is expanding generation and transmission capacity, capturing associated gas to fuel power plants instead of flaring it, interconnecting its grid with neighbours, and developing large-scale solar projects as part of a national push toward roughly 12 GW of solar by 2030.

How is the Kurdistan Region different for energy?

The Kurdistan Region operates its own energy regime, with a separate Ministry of Natural Resources, contracts and registration. Companies should decide early which regime they are entering, as it affects licensing and contracting.
