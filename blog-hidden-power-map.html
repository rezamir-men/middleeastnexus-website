The Hidden Power Map of Iraq's Economy: Who Really Drives the Market?

April 2026 — Baghdad · Reza Mir, Founder

To enter the Iraqi market successfully, reading official reports is not enough. Ministries, central bank statistics, and public tender announcements are only the visible layer of this economy. Behind that layer lies a network of informal actors in whose hands the fate of many contracts, licenses, and investment opportunities is decided.

This article is an attempt to map that hidden layer — not to expose it, but to help companies and investors who want to find a foothold in this market without the costly mistakes of newcomers.

Why Iraq's Economy Cannot Be Explained by Conventional Models

Post-2003 Iraq cannot be analysed through standard economic frameworks. Three structural features distinguish it from conventional regional markets:

First, the heavy dependence of the state budget on oil revenue (over 90%) means that the real flow of money in the economy moves through public spending and government contracts — not through a productive private sector.

Second, the ethno-sectarian power-sharing system (muhasasa) has effectively turned ministries and state institutions into quotas of political factions. Each ministry cultivates its own economic network.

Third, weak regulatory and judicial institutions have created vast space for intermediation, information rents, and informal dealings.

The result is the formation of four distinct networks of economic power, examined below.

Network One: Actors Tied to Shia Factions Close to Iran

This network has held the broadest influence in Iraq's federal economy since 2003, particularly over the past decade.

Main areas of activity: reconstruction contracts, imports of essential goods (fuel, food, pharmaceuticals), private banking, telecommunications, and infrastructure projects linked to ministries under the control of allied parties.

Key characteristic: reliance on political relationships to secure government contracts and, in many cases, connection to import chains from Iran or transit routes.

Consideration for foreign investors: direct entry into sectors influenced by this network — particularly for American or European firms — can carry the risk of US secondary sanctions. Rigorous compliance and KYC checks are essential before any partnership.

Network Two: The Kurdistan Regional Economy

The Kurdistan Region operates a relatively independent economy organised around two principal political families (Barzani and Talabani) and their affiliated networks.

Main areas of activity: oil and gas (especially independent contracts with foreign companies), construction, tourism, regional banking, and cross-border trade with Turkey and Iran.

Key characteristic: a quasi-familial structure, long-standing relationships with foreign partners (particularly Turkey and Gulf states), and relatively higher predictability than other regions.

Consideration for foreign investors: Kurdistan is often a lower-risk entry point for Western firms, but the legal disputes between Erbil and Baghdad over oil contracts have created significant legal risk. Every contract must be assessed under both jurisdictions.

Network Three: The Old Merchant Families of Baghdad and Basra

This group consists of the survivors of Iraq's traditional merchant class from the pre-2003 era who have managed to adapt to the new conditions.

Main areas of activity: traditional trade (electronics, automotive, food), currency exchange and money transfer, commercial real estate, and consumer goods imports.

Key characteristic: multi-generational accumulated capital, broad regional networks (particularly with Dubai, Jordan, and Turkey), and a conservative approach to politics. This group typically prefers to remain in the background.

Consideration for foreign investors: the best option for long-term commercial partnerships in non-strategic sectors. Reputation, market expertise, and ready distribution networks are the principal advantages of these partners.

Network Four: Post-2003 Emerging Actors

A generation of entrepreneurs who have grown over the past two decades through government contracts, services to international forces, or information technology.

Main areas of activity: logistics services, IT, telecommunications, media, and in recent years, digital startups and fintech.

Key characteristic: high flexibility, familiarity with international standards, while at the same time relying significantly on political relationships to maintain their position.

Consideration for foreign investors: the most dynamic segment of the market — but also the most volatile. The success of these actors is often tied to the durability of their political backers; a change of government can transform the standing of a major company within months.

The Role of Media as a Complementary Instrument of Power

An important point not to overlook in analysing Iraq's market is the integration of economic activity with media ownership. Many of the principal actors in all four networks also hold stakes in television channels, newspapers, or digital platforms.

This ownership is not merely an economic investment; it is a tool for shaping public narrative, pressuring commercial competitors, and protecting interests against journalistic investigation. For a foreign company, analysing the media profile of a potential partner is as important as financial due diligence.

Practical Recommendations for International Investors

Five principles drawn from two decades of operating in the Iraqi market:

1. Your local partner is your most important strategic decision. Choosing the wrong partner in Iraq sometimes proves more costly than choosing none at all. Do not underestimate the time and cost of due diligence.

2. Iraq's power map is dynamic. Every election cycle and every cabinet change can shift the balance of networks. Reliance on a single network creates systemic risk.

3. Transparency of your local partner's ownership structure is a red line. If you cannot identify the ultimate beneficial owners (UBO) of a counterparty, do not enter that contract.

4. Kurdistan and the federal centre are two markets, not one. Strategy, partners, and legal framework must be designed separately for each.

5. Physical presence and personal relationships are irreplaceable. Iraq is not a market that can be managed remotely through email and video conferences. Companies with permanent representatives on the ground make better decisions.

Conclusion

The Iraqi market offers real and substantial opportunities for investors — but those opportunities are hidden behind a complex informal structure. Understanding that structure is not a competitive advantage; it is a precondition for entry.

Companies that enter Iraq imagining they will find a "standard emerging market" typically exit within two years. Companies that take the time to understand the real map of economic power can build relationships that endure ten years or more.

The difference between these two groups lies not in capital, nor in technology — but in a deeper understanding of the very hidden layers this article has tried to illuminate.
