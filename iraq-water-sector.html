Market overview

A crisis-driven market moving fast

Iraq is facing one of its most serious water crises in decades. About 81% of the country's water originates upstream in Türkiye, and reduced river flows — combined with drought, ageing infrastructure and rising demand — have pushed salinity in the south to critical levels. In Basra, saltwater intrusion has triggered public-health problems, crop and livestock losses, and repeated protests.

The government's response has moved the market from talk to contracts. Iraq awarded around $10bn of water infrastructure between January 2025 and May 2026, against roughly $1.8bn in the previous three years. The flagship is the $2.42bn Al-Faw seawater desalination plant near Basra — designed for about 1 million cubic metres a day, with a transmission network to 11 cities and a dedicated 300MW power plant — alongside four further desalination plants approved for Basra in 2025. For international water companies, this is a rare combination: urgent need, real budgets, and a market still under-served by global players.

Key demand drivers

What is pushing the market

Salinity in the south

Saltwater intrusion in Basra and the south has made desalination a national priority, not an option.

Shrinking river flows

Around 81% of Iraq's water comes from upstream; reduced flows and drought are cutting freshwater supply.

Ageing, leaking networks

Up to 45% of water is lost in Basra through old and damaged pipework — a huge rehabilitation need.

Population growth

A young, growing and urbanising population is outpacing existing treatment and supply capacity.

Public-health pressure

Water-related illness and repeated protests are forcing faster government action and spending.

Industrial & power demand

Oil, industry and power generation all need reliable water, adding to municipal and agricultural demand.

These demand drivers provide a useful framework, but every opportunity still requires project-level commercial and regulatory assessment.

Buyers & decision-makers

Who actually awards the work

Water projects in Iraq cut across several authorities. Knowing which body leads a given scheme — and who inside it holds budget and sign-off — is often the difference between a live opportunity and wasted effort.

  • The Ministry of Water Resources, leading on rivers, dams, irrigation and major water policy.
  • The Ministry of Construction, Housing, Municipalities and Public Works, for municipal water and treatment.
  • Provincial governorates and local water directorates, controlling significant local delivery.
  • State-owned companies and public utilities operating plants and networks.
  • The National Investment Commission and provincial commissions, for investment-licence projects.
  • Financing vehicles such as the Iraqi–Chinese Fund and other bilateral mechanisms.
  • International organisations and donors funding water and sanitation programmes.
Water sits between several ministries and provincial bodies — mapping the real decision-maker for each project is exactly where local knowledge earns its value.
Procurement & contracting routes

How to actually win work

The real question for most international firms is not whether Iraq needs water infrastructure — it is how to secure a contract. Several routes exist, and the right one depends on your size, technology and appetite for risk.

Government tenders

Public competitive tenders from ministries, governorates and water directorates — the traditional route, requiring local registration.

EPC contracts

Engineering, procurement and construction packages for desalination and treatment plants, where international expertise is specifically sought.

EPC + finance (EPCF)

Contractor-arranged financing that eases pressure on the public budget — increasingly used for major water schemes.

Fund-backed projects

Projects financed through vehicles such as the Iraqi–Chinese Fund and other bilateral arrangements.

Direct supply

Supplying pumps, membranes, control systems, laboratory equipment, pipes or treatment chemicals into projects.

Local partner / agent

Working through a vetted Iraqi partner or agent that holds the local relationships and can tender on the ground.

Joint venture

A JV with an Iraqi contractor, combining international technology with local access and track record.

Operations & maintenance

O&M contracts on completed plants and networks — a longer-term, service-based way into the market.

Market entry risks

What to plan around

A realistic view of the risks is part of doing this well. Water projects in Iraq carry specific challenges — but they are manageable with the right preparation, partners and expectations.

  • A history of delays and cost escalation on major desalination schemes — experience and diligence matter.
  • Two distinct regimes — Federal Iraq and the Kurdistan Region — with different laws and tender systems.
  • The need to vet local partners carefully; the wrong partner is a bigger risk than the market itself.
  • Financing, payment terms and guarantees that must be planned before, not during, a contract.
  • Project timing tied to public budgets and, in some cases, bilateral financing arrangements.
  • Water availability and upstream flows that are themselves affected by regional politics.
Being honest about these is deliberate: the firms that succeed in Iraq are the ones that plan around its realities rather than around headlines.

FAQ

Common questions

Who awards water projects in Iraq?

Water projects are awarded by the Ministry of Water Resources, the Ministry of Construction, Housing, Municipalities and Public Works, provincial governorates and water directorates, state-owned companies, and the National and provincial Investment Commissions. Large desalination schemes are increasingly delivered through EPC contracts and investment funds.

Can foreign companies bid directly on Iraqi water projects?

Foreign companies can bid, and international firms are actively sought for large desalination and treatment projects. Companies usually register a local company or branch, or partner with an Iraqi contractor, and can license qualifying projects under Investment Law No. 13.

Is a local partner required for water projects in Iraq?

A local partner is not always legally required, but a vetted Iraqi partner, agent or subcontractor is often essential in practice for tendering, permits, mobilisation and navigating the Ministry of Water Resources and local water directorates.

How are Iraqi water projects financed?

Financing comes from the federal budget, provincial budgets, the Iraqi–Chinese Fund and other bilateral vehicles, investment-licence projects, and EPC-plus-finance structures. Some major desalination projects have been linked to dedicated financing mechanisms.

Which governorates have the highest water demand in Iraq?

Basra has the most acute need due to severe salinity and its desalination programme, followed by the southern governorates of Dhi Qar and Maysan, and Baghdad for urban water and wastewater. Central provinces such as Babylon need irrigation and treatment.
