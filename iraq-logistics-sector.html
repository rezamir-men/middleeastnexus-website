Executive summary

The opportunity in brief

The market
Iraq is building a new east–west trade corridor: the ~$17bn Development Road, a 1,200 km rail and road link from the Grand Faw Port to the Turkish border, positioned to offer a roughly 10-day advantage over the Suez Canal for Asia–Europe freight.

Ports, railways, highways, industrial cities, dry ports and the freight and logistics operations that will run on them — one of the largest single infrastructure and logistics programmes in the region.

Separate Federal and Kurdistan regimes, long project timelines and phasing, financing and payment structures, customs and border-process reform still underway, and security along parts of the route.

Market overview

A country repositioning as a trade bridge

For decades Iraq's geography — sitting between the Gulf and Türkiye, on the shortest land route from Asia toward Europe — was an underused asset. The Development Road is the national bid to change that: a 1,200 km rail and road corridor, estimated at around $17bn, running from the Grand Faw Port on the Gulf coast north through the length of the country to the Turkish border, and on into Europe via Türkiye's networks.

At its southern end, the Grand Faw Port is under construction as one of the largest container ports in the region, designed to receive the biggest vessels and to serve as the corridor's terminal. Along the route, industrial cities, dry ports and logistics hubs are planned. The World Bank estimates the corridor could attract up to 14 million tonnes of international and 20 million tonnes of regional freight by 2040, generating around $4bn a year and creating at least 100,000 jobs. For international ports operators, rail and civil contractors, freight and logistics firms and equipment suppliers, this is a rare, corridor-scale opportunity being built largely from scratch.

Buyers & decision-makers

Who actually awards the work

Iraq's logistics programme sits largely with central transport institutions, but provincial and investment bodies matter too. Identifying the right authority for a given package is essential before investing effort.

  • The Ministry of Transport, leading the Development Road, ports and railways.
  • The General Company for Ports of Iraq, operating Umm Qasr and the ports network.
  • The Iraqi Republic Railways Company, for rail modernisation and new links.
  • The Grand Faw Port authority and project management for the flagship port.
  • Provincial governorates along the corridor, for local infrastructure and permits.
  • The National Investment Commission and provincial commissions, for investment-licence projects.
  • Central government and international partners financing the corridor.
On a programme this large and multi-layered, knowing which body owns a given package — and who inside it can commit — is exactly where local knowledge earns its value.
Procurement & contracting routes

How to actually win work

There are several ways into Iraq's logistics sector, depending on whether you build, operate, supply or finance.

Government tenders

Public tenders from the Ministry of Transport and its bodies for works, systems and services.

EPC contracts

Engineering, procurement and construction packages for ports, rail and road infrastructure.

Contractor-arranged financing for large corridor packages, easing pressure on the public budget.

Concessions & PPP

BOT / operate

Direct supply

Joint venture

Market entry risks

What to plan around

A realistic view of the risks is part of doing this well. None of these are reasons to avoid Iraq — they are factors to plan around, and most are manageable with the right preparation and partners.

  • Long timelines and phasing — the corridor runs to 2028, 2033 and 2050, so packages come in waves.
  • Two distinct regimes — Federal Iraq and the Kurdistan Region — with different laws and tender systems.
  • The need to vet local partners carefully; the wrong partner is a bigger risk than the market itself.
  • Financing, payment terms and guarantees that must be planned before, not during, a contract.
  • Customs and border processes still being reformed, which affect freight operations.
  • Security and logistics that vary along the route and require sensible planning.
Being honest about these is deliberate: the firms that succeed in Iraq are the ones that plan around its realities rather than around headlines.

FAQ

Common questions

What is Iraq's Development Road?

The Development Road is a roughly $17bn, 1,200 km rail and road corridor planned to run from the Grand Faw Port on Iraq's Gulf coast to the Turkish border, connecting the Gulf to Türkiye and Europe. It aims to turn Iraq into a transit hub and is being delivered in phases to 2028, 2033 and 2050.

Who awards logistics and transport projects in Iraq?

Projects are led by the Ministry of Transport and its bodies (including the ports and railways companies), the General Company for Ports of Iraq, provincial governorates, and the National and provincial Investment Commissions. Major corridor projects involve central government and international partners.

Can foreign companies work on Iraqi ports and transport projects?

Yes. International port operators, EPC contractors, rail and logistics firms are actively sought for the Grand Faw Port, the Development Road and related infrastructure, typically through tenders, EPC contracts, concessions or partnerships, and by registering locally.

What is the Grand Faw Port?

The Grand Faw Port is a deep-water port under construction on Iraq's Gulf coast near Basra, intended to become one of the largest container ports in the region and the southern terminal of the Development Road corridor.

Which governorates matter most for logistics in Iraq?

Basra is central, as home to the Grand Faw Port and Umm Qasr. The Development Road then runs north through Dhi Qar, Najaf/Karbala, Baghdad and Nineveh (Mosul) to the Turkish border, making each a logistics opportunity.

How is the Development Road financed?

The programme combines Iraqi government funding with international partners and financing vehicles; stage-one railway works have been supported by World Bank engagement, and regional partners have been discussed as strategic financiers. Individual packages use tenders, EPC, EPCF and concession structures.
