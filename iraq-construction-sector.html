Market overview

A large, reconstruction-driven market

Iraq is undergoing a sustained rebuild after years of conflict, funded by substantial oil-backed public budgets and, increasingly, private and foreign investment. The construction sector spans residential, commercial, industrial, institutional and infrastructure work — from mass housing and new cities to schools, hospitals, roads, ports and the buildings that support the wider reconstruction effort.

At the September 2025 Iraq Investment Forum, the government set out investment opportunities worth around $450 billion across more than 160 projects, and vehicles such as the Iraq Development Fund are being used to draw in local, regional and international investors. For international contractors and suppliers, the scale is matched by a market that is still under-served by global firms — leaving room for those who understand how to operate in it.

Key demand drivers

What is pushing the market

A young, growing population

Roughly 46 million people and rising, driving structural demand for housing, schools, hospitals and services.

A deep housing deficit

At least 2.5 million homes short, with up to 3.5 million additional units needed to keep pace with growth.

Post-conflict reconstruction

Rebuilding of liberated and damaged areas, from Mosul and Nineveh to towns across the centre and north.

Rapid urbanisation

Migration to cities is expanding Baghdad, Basra, Erbil and other centres, straining existing housing and infrastructure.

Oil-backed public budgets

Large capital budgets channel public spending into transport, housing and public buildings year after year.

New investment vehicles

The Iraq Development Fund, investment licences and developer-led models are opening the market to foreign firms.

Buyers & decision-makers

Who actually awards the work

One of the most common mistakes foreign firms make in Iraq is misreading who holds the authority to commit. Depending on the project, the buyer may sit at federal, provincial or local level — or be a private developer or state-owned company. Identifying the real decision-maker early is often the difference between a live opportunity and a dead end.

  • Federal ministries — including Construction, Housing & Municipalities, and sector ministries for schools, health and transport.
  • Provincial governorates and municipalities, which control significant local budgets and permits.
  • The National Investment Commission and provincial investment commissions, for investment-licence projects.
  • State-owned companies and public bodies commissioning their own facilities.
  • Private developers delivering large residential and mixed-use schemes.
  • Main contractors and consultants awarding subcontracts and supply packages.
  • International organisations and donors funding reconstruction and housing.
Knowing which of these bodies is empowered to commit on a given project — and who inside it — is exactly where local knowledge earns its value.
Procurement & contracting routes

How to actually win work

For most international firms the real question is not whether Iraq needs construction — it is how to secure a contract. There are several routes in, and the right one depends on your size, sector and appetite for risk.

Government tenders

Public competitive tenders issued by ministries, governorates and state companies — the traditional route, requiring local registration and documentation.

EPC contracts

Engineering, procurement and construction packages for larger works, often where international expertise is specifically sought.

EPC + finance (EPCF)

Contractor-arranged financing models that ease pressure on the public budget — increasingly common for large schemes.

Developer-led investment

Investment-licence projects where a developer builds, and sometimes finances and operates, under Law No. 13 incentives.

Direct supply

Supplying materials, equipment or systems into projects, either directly or through distributors.

Local partner / agent

Working through a vetted Iraqi partner or agent that holds the local relationships and can tender on the ground.

Joint venture

A JV with an Iraqi contractor, combining international capability with local access and track record.

Subcontracting

Taking defined packages under a main contractor already delivering a large project — a lower-risk way to enter.

Market entry risks

What to plan around

A realistic view of the risks is part of doing this well. None of these are reasons to avoid Iraq — they are factors to plan around, and most are manageable with the right preparation and partners.

  • Two distinct regimes — Federal Iraq and the Kurdistan Region — with different laws, registration and tender systems.
  • The need to vet local partners carefully; the wrong partner is a bigger risk than the market itself.
  • Payment terms, advance/retention structures and guarantees that must be planned before, not during, a contract.
  • Company registration, tax and customs processes that reward preparation and local guidance.
  • Project scope and timing that can shift, particularly where funding is tied to the public budget cycle.
  • Security and logistics that vary by governorate and require sensible movement planning.
Being honest about these is deliberate: the firms that succeed in Iraq are the ones that plan around its realities rather than around headlines.

FAQ

Common questions

Who awards construction and housing projects in Iraq?

Projects are awarded by federal ministries (such as Construction, Housing and Municipalities), provincial governorates and municipalities, state-owned companies, and the National and provincial Investment Commissions. Large housing schemes are increasingly delivered by private developers under investment licences.

Can foreign construction companies bid directly in Iraq?

Foreign companies can bid, but usually register a local company or branch, or partner with an Iraqi contractor. Investment Law No. 13 provides a licensing route with tax and customs incentives for qualifying projects.

Is a local partner required for construction work in Iraq?

A local partner is not always legally required, but a vetted Iraqi partner, agent or subcontractor is often essential in practice for tendering, permits, mobilisation and navigating local authorities.

How are Iraqi housing and construction projects financed?

Financing comes from the federal capital budget, provincial budgets, the Iraq Development Fund, investment-licence projects, developer-led models, and EPC-plus-finance (EPCF) structures that ease pressure on the public budget.

What is the difference between Federal Iraq and the Kurdistan Region for construction?

Federal Iraq and the Kurdistan Region operate separate investment laws, registration processes and tender systems. Companies should decide early which regime they are entering, as it affects ownership, licensing and contracting.

Which governorates have the most construction activity?

Baghdad is the largest single market, with major activity also in Basra, Erbil, and reconstruction-heavy Nineveh (Mosul), plus pilgrimage-driven building in Karbala and Najaf.
